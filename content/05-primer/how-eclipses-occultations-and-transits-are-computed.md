::: summary
- **Every event is a body casting a shadow.** The light can come straight from a source, the Sun or a star, or be reflected by a lit body such as a planet. A blocking body casts a shadow in that light.
- **Who stands in the shadow decides what happens.** If the observer stands in it, the observer sees the source hidden: every occultation, every transit and every solar eclipse is this case. If another body stands in it, that body goes dark for everyone: lunar eclipses and the eclipses of planetary satellites.
- **The hidden source is a point or a disc.** A star is a point, so the shadow is a cylinder and the star vanishes at once. The Sun, a planet or a satellite is a disc, so the shadow is a cone with an umbra, a penumbra and an antumbra, which give total, partial and annular events [@bes-es1961] [@occ-es1961].
- **This is the Explanatory Supplement's own split.** Its eclipse is a shadow falling on a body, and its occultation is a block in the line of sight. It notes that a solar eclipse is really an occultation [@prim-es1992-terms].
:::

## Every event is a shadow

A blocking body casts a shadow in whatever light falls on it. The light may
come straight from a source that shines itself, the Sun or a star, or from a
body that the Sun lights, such as a planet or a satellite. There are two
cases, and they differ in who stands in the shadow:

- **the observer**, who then sees the source hidden behind the blocking
  body; or
- **another body**, which then loses its sunlight and goes dark.

The two sections below take each in turn. In every diagram each body has two
labels: the part it plays, and what it is in that example.

## Hidden: the observer is in the shadow

When the observer stands in a blocking body's shadow, the observer sees the
source of that light hidden behind the body. This is an [?occultation].
Someone standing outside the shadow sees nothing happen, so the time depends
on where the observer stands, and a prediction is a path or a map [@occ-es1961].

![In a lunar occultation of a star the observer stands in the Moon's shadow in starlight, so the star is hidden. Observers outside that band see nothing happen.](img/case-hidden.svg)

A star is so far away that its light arrives in parallel lines, so the
Moon's shadow in starlight is a cylinder exactly as wide as the Moon. It
darkens nothing, but it is how these events are computed: an occultation is
predicted as the path of that shadow across the Earth [@occ-es1961]
[@mb-gomes-junior-2022-sora].

The hidden source can be a disc rather than a point. Then the shadow is a
cone, and the event can be total, partial or annular. A solar eclipse is
this case, with the Sun as the source and the Moon as the blocking body: the
observer in the Moon's umbra sees the whole Sun hidden. That is why the
Supplement calls a solar eclipse really an occultation [@prim-es1992-terms].

![In a solar eclipse the observer stands in the Moon's umbra, so the Moon hides the whole Sun: being in the shadow and seeing the Sun hidden are one event.](img/example-solar-eclipse.svg)

A [?transit] is the same with a body far too small to cover the source.
Venus's umbra closes long before it reaches the Earth, so the observer stands
in its antumbra and sees a dot cross the Sun [@tr-es1961].

![The observer stands in Venus's antumbra and sees a small dark dot on the Sun: a transit of Venus.](img/example-venus-transit.svg)

The hidden source can also be a body that the Sun lights rather than one
that shines. Then its phase shapes the outline that the blocking body hides.
Chauvenet treats a planet hidden by the Moon this way [@bes-chauvenet-1863].

![The Sun lights Io, and the observer stands in Jupiter's shadow in Io's reflected light, so Io is hidden behind Jupiter.](img/example-satellite-occultation.svg)

![An asteroid's shadow in starlight crosses the Earth as a narrow band. Only observers inside that band see the star disappear.](img/example-asteroid-occultation.svg)

- The Moon **hides the Sun**, a solar eclipse [@bes-es1961].
- Mercury or Venus **crosses the Sun**, a transit [@tr-es1961].
- The Moon **hides a star or a planet** [@occ-es1961].
- An **asteroid or a trans-Neptunian object hides a star**. Only observers
  inside a narrow band on the Earth see it [@mb-gomes-junior-2022-sora].
- A planet **hides its own satellite**, and a satellite **crosses** its
  planet [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].
- One satellite **hides another**, a mutual occultation
  [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].
- One planet **hides another**, which last happened in 1818
  [@mb-gray-mutual].

![When played, the Moon's shadow slides across the Earth while, on the right, the Moon slides across the Sun as the observer at the dot sees it. The Sun is fully covered only while the umbra is on the observer. The still frame is totality.](img/anim-solar-eclipse.svg){.anim}

## Darkened: another body is in the shadow

When another body stands in the blocking body's shadow, the sunlight
reaching it is cut off and it goes dark. This is an [?eclipse] in the strict
sense. Everyone who can see the darkened body sees it at the same instant,
apart from the time light takes to reach them, so a prediction is a list of
times rather than a map of paths. Where the observer stands only decides
whether the body is above their horizon [@lun-5mcle-tp2009].

![In a lunar eclipse the Earth blocks the sunlight on its way to the Moon, so the Moon goes dark for everyone on the Earth's night side, wherever they stand.](img/case-darkened.svg)

![Jupiter blocks the sunlight on its way to Io, so Io goes dark for every observer who can see it.](img/example-satellite-eclipse.svg)

![Io blocks the sunlight on its way to a patch of Jupiter's cloud tops, which shows as a dark spot: a shadow transit.](img/example-shadow-transit.svg)

- A **lunar eclipse** is the Moon in the Earth's shadow
  [@lun-5mcle-tp2009].
- An **eclipse of a satellite** is, for example, Io in Jupiter's shadow
  [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].
- A **shadow transit** is a satellite's shadow falling on its planet's cloud
  tops, a patch of the planet in eclipse [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].
- A **mutual eclipse** is one satellite in another satellite's shadow
  [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].

![When played, the Moon crosses the Earth's shadow and its copper tint deepens in the umbra, where only sunlight bent through the Earth's atmosphere reaches it. The still frame is totality.](img/anim-lunar-eclipse.svg){.anim}

## A planet and its satellites: both cases

A planet with satellites shows both cases in one orbit. When Io enters
Jupiter's shadow, Io is darkened. When the observer stands in Jupiter's
shadow in Io's reflected light, Io is hidden behind Jupiter. When Io's
shadow falls on Jupiter, a patch of cloud tops is darkened. When the
observer stands in Io's shadow in Jupiter's light, Io crosses in front of
Jupiter [@jup-imcce-phenjupiter-readme].

![When played, Io goes once round Jupiter and passes the four places where each kind of event happens. The angle between the Sun and the Earth is exaggerated to separate them. The still frame shows Io in Jupiter's shadow.](img/anim-io-orbit.svg){.anim}

## The shape of a shadow

The source's size decides the shadow's shape. The Sun is a disc, so a body
casts a cone with three parts [@bes-es1961]:

- the [?umbra], where the whole Sun is hidden and the event is **total**;
- the [?antumbra], beyond the point where the umbra closes, where the body
  looks smaller than the Sun and the event is **annular**;
- the [?penumbra], where part of the Sun is hidden and the event is
  **partial**.

A transit is the annular case with a body far too small to cover the Sun:
the observer is in the planet's antumbra and sees a dot on the Sun
[@tr-es1961]. A star is a point, so its shadow is a cylinder with no
penumbra. The star does not fade but vanishes at once, apart from faint
diffraction fringes [@occ-es1961].

![The source's size decides the shadow's shape: the Sun gives a cone whose umbra, penumbra and antumbra give total, partial and annular views, and a star gives a cylinder with no penumbra, so it vanishes at once.](img/shadow-shape.svg)

The computation follows the same steps for every event. Choose a plane
perpendicular to the shadow, the [?fundamental-plane]. Find the body's
shadow on it as a circle. Find the observer, or the darkened body, on it as
a point. An event begins and ends when the point crosses the circle
[@bes-es1961] [@occ-es1961].

## How the Explanatory Supplement names them

The 1992 Explanatory Supplement uses four names [@prim-es1992-terms]:

- an **eclipse**, when one body passes into the shadow of another;
- an **occultation**, when a larger body passes in front of a smaller one;
- a **transit**, when a smaller body passes in front of a larger one;
- a **shadow transit**, when the shadow of a smaller body passes in front of
  a larger one.

Its chapter on eclipses draws the line between shadow and line of sight. It
adds that long observation from the Earth gave the events their names before
the distinction was understood [@prim-es1992-terms]. In the terms of this
page, its eclipse and its shadow transit are the darkened case. Its
occultation and its transit are the hidden case, told apart only by which
body looks larger. A solar eclipse is an occultation of the Sun, seen from
inside the Moon's shadow.

The name of this site, "Computing Eclipses and Occultations", uses the two
broad terms. An eclipse is the darkened case, and an occultation is the
hidden case. A transit is an occultation in which the nearer body looks
smaller than the body behind it, so the site name does not list it apart.
The Supplement's own section heading names all three, "Eclipses,
Occultations, and Transits", because it keeps the traditional names
[@prim-es1992-terms].
